A dither tool
for anything

Round one found a look that only works on engravings. This round is about a tool: point it at any photo or any footage and get a dithered picture that reads, holds still and keeps your colours.

The engine has three stages, each swappable: prep (get the picture ready), method (turn tone into pattern) and palette (2 to 4 colours). Code in engine.py, experiments in round2.py. Click any still for full size.

01Prep decides whether a photo reads

Same method (blue noise), same three-tone sepia, three preps. none is the photo as it comes. levels stretches the darkest and lightest percent to the ends. punch adds local contrast (a wide unsharp mask), a gentle S-curve and a little sharpening. Round one fed photos in raw; that's most of why they looked muddy.

Punch, every time. Faces get their shape back, signs and edges come through, and nothing looks processed once it's dithered. It's the default.

02Methods

All with punch prep and three tones. Floyd-Steinberg and Atkinson diffuse the error to neighbours: smooth, organic, the best stills. Bayer is the regular cross-hatch grid of old computers. Blue noise is a fixed grain with no pattern you can see. Halftone is round dots on a 45° screen, like print. Lines is a horizontal line screen, which bands badly with three tones.

03Three tones fix the mud

Your colour pairs, plus three- and four-tone ramps in the same families. Two tones close together is what made round one murky; a third, lighter tone gives highlights somewhere to go without losing the mood.

04On everything

Every photo from round one plus two Doré plates, with the default (punch, blue noise, sepia in three tones), and Floyd-Steinberg next to it for comparison.

This is the answer to "it only works on engravings". Portraits, food, a cat, a concert, a night street, a spacewalk and a Doré plate all sit in the same language now.

05Footage

Four public-domain and CC clips, six seconds each: an astronaut in a cluttered module, horses in a flat field, a train pulling into a lit station, open sea. Each is dithered frame by frame by five methods, in three-tone sepia.

Boil is what matters: the share of pixels that flip in places where the picture didn't change (source moved less than 1%). Real motion makes pixels change; boil is the extra.

Hysteresis is the new trick: a pixel keeps last frame's tone unless its value has clearly moved past the threshold. It cuts Floyd-Steinberg's boil by 10 to 25 times and keeps its smooth look. It needs the previous frame, so it works in an offline tool (footage in, footage out) and not inside a HyperFrames render, which can ask for any frame in any order.

The grids below are 1:1 centre crops, so you see real pixels. Top row: the prepped source, Floyd-Steinberg, FS + hysteresis. Bottom row: Bayer 8, blue noise, halftone.

Plain Floyd-Steinberg is out for anything that moves. Hysteresis rescues it for footage; the threshold methods are stable by construction and are what goes into compositions. Waves are the hard case for hysteresis (everything moves a little all the time), so the stable fixed patterns win there.

06What I'd build

  • ditherPass in kino: any source (canvas painting, photo, or a <video>) through prep, a threshold method (blue noise, Bayer, halftone) and a 2 to 4 tone palette, at 1×, 2× or 3× pixels. Seek-safe, so it renders like everything else. Replaces the current ditherCanvas, which is two colours and Bayer only.
  • scripts/dither.py: footage in, footage out, with the same settings plus Floyd-Steinberg with hysteresis. For dithering real video before it goes into a piece, or as a finished thing on its own.
  • Palette presets in the looks: sepia, ice, prussian, oxblood, phosphor, paper, in two to four tones.
  • Then a short piece with real footage and photos mixed, cut to music, to see it all hold together.
