kino · four styles · one song · made overnight

Four styles,
one song

Four 27-second pieces on the same cut of "The Future of Speech": blueprint, oscilloscope, risograph and teletext. Same track, same beat grid, four completely different machines underneath. Each one is a real way pictures used to be made (a print process, a measuring instrument, a broadcast system), rebuilt in code and then pushed to do something it was never for: carry a song.

Every tab has the video, then what I chose and why, which kino pieces it used, what I had to invent, and what I learned. All four were rendered at 2560×1440 for your screen, in parallel.

One cross-cutting lesson from the render times: risograph took about 2 minutes, the other three about 45 each. The difference is screenPass, which samples the picture 24 times per pixel for its lens blur, plus bloom. In software rendering that's the whole bill. A cheaper blur (two separable passes instead of a 24-tap disc) is the obvious next optimisation.

25 S · 2560×1440 · 30 FPScomposition compositions/screen-test

Cyanotype

The one that started it: the NousCon teaser's look, rebuilt from the teardown and tried on our own material. Sun-printed photos, Prussian blue, filmed off a Trinitron that was never there. It came before the other four, so it's tab zero.

The idea

The teardown showed the NousCon screen is synthetic: the aperture grille is in phase across all three colours, the grade is one luma-to-blue curve, and the blur only falls off left and right. So instead of filming a CRT we built the screen as a pass, then fed it photos it had never seen (Commons plates: a mouth, a microphone, a switchboard, a dish, a crowd) to check the look holds up off the reference.

Shot list

  • 0:00Plotter: the song's spectral history draws line by line, the title wipes in by word.
  • 0:03The plotter slides off and a contact strip of sunprints slides through. "SPEECH".
  • 0:09Torn wipe to the dish. "LISTENING".
  • 0:14A lens pass opens the network inside a glass circle; it grows one generation per beat.
  • 0:20Torn wipe back to the lockup over the sea.

Choices

  • Physical transitions only. Slides, tears, a lens. Things a print or a piece of glass could do; no crossfades.
  • Whole words, not letters. The first try revealed letter by letter and was hard to read. Now type wipes in word by word.
  • Measured, not eyeballed. Grille pitch, ramp and stripe contrast were matched to the NousCon frames numerically, within about 10%.

This render is the one you saw first and still has the water drop between the dish and the network. The code doesn't any more; a fresh render is about 45 minutes.

Pieces

  • NEWscreenPass: gradient-map ramp, mean-preserving grille, lens softness, green fringe, bloom, grain.
  • NEWpreparePlate and sunprint: a photo levelled for print inside a brushed cyanotype edge.
  • NEWprintStrip, tornWipe, lensPass, networkTree, wipeText, caption.
  • USEDspectrumFeed as the plotter, beatMap for the bar cuts.

What I learned

Most of a "CRT look" is the grade, not the screen. The ramp alone gets you 80% of the way; the grille only reads when it doesn't darken the picture, which is why it had to be mean-preserving. The fringe has to shift the picture and leave the grille alone, or the stripes turn green and magenta. Still missing: the fine woven speckle in the NousCon close-ups, which our softness blurs away.

Blueprint

One engineering drawing for the song, and a camera that travels across it. There are no cuts at all: the transitions are camera moves, and each detail draws itself as the camera arrives, like watching someone draft it.

The idea

A blueprint is a document about how something is built, so the song gets a sheet about how speech is built: the signal path from voice to speaker, an exploded microphone, a loudspeaker in section, and the song itself plotted as data. The title block in the corner, which every real drawing has, is left empty until the very end, then the title writes itself into it. That's the payoff shot.

The jokes are in the drafting conventions: SCALE: NTS, DRAWN: BERKER-Z, a notes block reading "SIGNAL IS SPEECH. SPEECH IS SIGNAL." and "DO NOT SCALE DRAWING."

Shot list

  • 0:00Wide on the sheet. Grid sweeps in, the border, zone marks, title block frame and signal path draw themselves.
  • 0:02Camera glides to Fig. 1. The microphone draws in part by part, then on each beat of the next bar one part flies off its axis: grille, capsule, shock mount, connector and cable. Numbered callouts and the parts list fill in as each part moves.
  • 0:07Glide to Fig. 2, the driver in section. The magnet assembly hatches in, then the cone starts moving with the actual bass of the track, with a live "EXCURSION +x MM" readout.
  • 0:10On the surge, a slow push in on the speaker for two bars.
  • 0:16Glide to Fig. 3: the song as a spectral section, our ridgelines on frequency and time axes.
  • 0:21Pull back to the whole finished sheet; the notes write in.
  • 0:23Push into the title block. "THE FUTURE OF SPEECH" is drawn in outline, then filled.

Choices

  • Camera instead of cuts. You wanted movement in every shot. Here it's built into the concept: the sheet never stops being one object, and the camera is always either gliding or drifting slowly.
  • Everything draws itself. Lines, arcs, rectangles, hatching, dimension lines and text all take a progress value, so a detail can appear the way a pen would make it.
  • Beats move the drawing, nothing flashes. The explode is one part per beat, the signal-path pulse hops one stage per beat, the cone follows the bass.
  • Real blueprint colour. A ramp from dark table to Prussian paper to near-white lines, paper mottling and fibres, two fold creases (the sheet was folded in four), a little ink bleed. No screen grille: it's a print.

Pieces

  • USEDscreenPass as a print pass: blueprint ramp, grille off, softness for ink bleed.
  • USEDspectrumFeed for Fig. 3, with its new ground option so the occlusion fill matches the paper instead of black.
  • USEDwipeText (now with an outline mode) for the title, beatMap for the grid.
  • NEWA pen kit: polyP, arcP, rectP, ellipseP, hatch, dim, balloon. Every primitive strokes the first p of its length.
  • NEWA sheet camera: poses with eased glides between them and a slow drift while holding, zoom interpolated in log space so it feels even.
  • NEWBass follower: the low bands of the spectrum, smoothed, driving a physical part.

What I learned

A drawing that draws itself is very forgiving: anything that appears in the order a person would draft it reads as intentional, even simple geometry. The speaker is a handful of lines and rectangles, and the hatching and the section arrows are what make it read as engineering. Conventions do most of the work.

A moving camera over thin lines is exactly the shimmer problem from the ridgelines, and it doesn't fully go away. What helps is lines at least two or three screen pixels wide, a bit of blur from the pass (which also reads as ink bleed), and glides that are slow, eased and never stop abruptly. The wide shot at 0.4× zoom is the weakest moment for this; the close-ups hold up.

Worth taking into the library: the pen kit and the sheet camera. Both are style-agnostic; a camera roaming over one big canvas would suit maps, diagrams, a timeline, anything that's one document rather than a sequence of shots.

Oscilloscope

The song drawn by an electron beam on an amber phosphor tube. Every trace is the actual audio, sample by sample, not an animation of it. The scope changes mode with the song's structure, and the picture squashes to a line and reopens between modes, like turning the mode knob.

The idea

A scope is the most honest picture of sound there is. So instead of decorating the song, this one just looks at it, four different ways, and lets the music be the drawing. It's the natural sibling of the ridgelines: same data, a different instrument.

Modes

  • 0:00Y-T sweep, triggered on the beat. The trace runs across the screen once per beat, the previous sweep fading behind it. The title is written by the beam.
  • 0:05Phase portrait. The signal plotted against itself 2.1 and 4.2 ms later, in 3D, rotating at a constant rate. Tones make loops, noise makes fur, so you can see the texture of the sound.
  • 0:10Polar. The waveform wrapped around a circle that breathes with the bass.
  • 0:16Lissajous. Two tones locked to the tempo; the frequency ratio changes on every beat (1:2, 2:3, 3:4, 3:5…) and morphs into the next, the phase drifting so the figure seems to turn.
  • 0:21Sweep again, the title written large, then the classic CRT power-off: the picture collapses to a line, then to a dot that fades.

Choices

  • Amber, not green. Green phosphor is the cliché (and you've told me about my lime habit). P3 amber is warmer and reads as an old measuring instrument rather than a hacker movie.
  • Light adds up. The beam is drawn three times (wide and faint, medium, thin and hot) with additive blending, so where the trace crosses itself it gets brighter, the way phosphor does.
  • Persistence is computed, not accumulated. HyperFrames seeks frames out of order, so "the last frame" doesn't exist. Afterglow is drawn by re-plotting the previous few windows of audio at decaying intensity, which gives the same look and stays deterministic.
  • Mode changes are physical. The picture squashes vertically to a line and reopens, like the scope re-triggering. No cuts, no flashes.
  • A bulging tube. I gave screenPass a second curvature axis so the glass bulges both ways, with heavy bloom and no grille.

Pieces

  • USEDscreenPass with an amber ramp, beatMap, the spectrum for bass.
  • NEWcurveY on screenPass: tube curvature in both axes, and anything bent off the glass goes black.
  • NEWRaw audio as data: the track decoded to int8 at 24 kHz (both channels, base64 in assets/scope.js, 1.7 MB) so the beam can draw real samples.
  • NEWA beam renderer (three-pass additive stroke) and beam text.
  • NEWAuto gain, like a scope's auto-set: RMS over half a second, sampled in quarter-second steps so it doesn't pump.

What I learned

The first pass had the quiet intro drawing a flat band and the phase portrait as a tiny speck: real audio has a huge dynamic range, and a scope that's right for the chorus is useless for the intro. Auto gain fixed it, but it has to be slow and stepped, or the picture breathes with every word.

The Lissajous section is the only part that isn't the audio, and it's the most beautiful, which is a useful lesson: pure, tempo-locked maths gives a clean counterpoint to the messy real signal around it. The phase portrait is the most surprising; delay embedding is a trick from chaos theory and I'd use it again for any "show me the shape of this sound" moment.

Worth taking into the library: the beam renderer, the raw-sample asset format and auto gain. Together they're an audioScope primitive.

Risograph

A two-colour fan zine in fluorescent pink and blue on cream, as one print run. The zine is a single tall sheet coming off the drum at a constant speed and never turns a page. The two inks move on their own: pink drifts out of register during each bar and snaps back onto the blue on every downbeat.

Take one, and why it didn't work

The first version was five designed pages with a paper-feed transition between them. You called it right: that's a slideshow. The pages had motion inside them, but the piece itself didn't move. It went page, wipe, page.

The ones that worked (blueprint, scope, teletext) all have the motion built into the concept: one sheet and a roaming camera, a beam that never stops drawing, a TV loading pages. So take two takes the most physical thing about a riso, paper going through a drum, and makes that the whole film.

The run, top to bottom

  • 0:00Cover. Stacked title, "OF SPEECH" on the pink drum, a pink halftone bubble that swells with the bass.
  • 0:04The singer. Blue halftone portrait, pink bubbles breathing on her, a pink band, a column of text.
  • 0:09Contact sheet. Six frames, pink borders, a pink marquee sliding sideways at its own speed.
  • 0:13Type wall. Blue rows of SPEECH fixed to the paper, pink rows sliding against them, purple wherever they cross.
  • 0:17The microphone. Pink rings leave it one per beat. "Every word leaves at 343 metres a second."
  • 0:21Back cover. The run slows and stops, and both drums lock into register for good.

How it moves

  • Constant scroll in whole pixels. 11 px per frame, no easing until the end, so the paper reads as machinery. Integer steps keep the halftone from crawling.
  • Misregistration is the beat. Pink swings up to 150 px off and back on a sine across each bar, alternating direction. It lands exactly on the downbeat, so the composed image locks once a bar. Registration marks in both margins make it readable: you watch the pink cross drift off its blue twin and land back on it.
  • The bass changes dot size, not position: blobs and bubbles swell, so the halftone itself breathes.
  • Things on the paper still move: the marquee, the pink type rows, the rings.

Pieces

  • USEDrisoPass (new last night), preparePlate, beatMap, the spectrum for bass.
  • NEWA print-run scroll: integer-pixel constant speed with an eased stop timed so the last section lands framed.
  • NEWPer-ink transforms: each separation gets its own offset every frame. The register drift is just two numbers for the pink drum, but it turns the print process itself into the motion.
  • NEWRegistration marks as a readable instrument for the drift.

What I learned

Good pages don't make a good video. The motion has to come from the medium's own physics. For a riso that's the paper path and the two drums, so the drift and the scroll aren't decoration. They're the riso doing what a riso does, exaggerated and put on the beat.

A per-beat "drum lurch" (blue jumping 9 px sideways and settling) was in the first render of take two. It read as jerky left-right jitter and came straight out. Beats can push things, but only with smooth motion, never an instant jump.

Two inks sliding over each other is the most riso-looking thing there is. The purple overlap moves constantly, which keeps even a still part of the sheet alive.

The file is large (310 MB): a halftone screen over constantly moving content changes every pixel every frame, which is close to the worst case for video compression. Delivery files for this style want a higher bitrate budget than the others. A web version would need a softer encode.

Teletext

A 1980s broadcast text service for the song. Forty by twenty-five cells, eight colours, characters made of chunky pixels and pictures made of 2×3 blocks. Pages load the way they did: the page number rolls in the header while the set searches, then the new page paints in row by row.

The idea

Teletext is the most constrained picture system there ever was, and the constraint is the style. So the piece is a little teletext service about the song: an index page, a picture page, a live spectrum, page 888 subtitles over the singer, and a credits page. The transitions are teletext's own: you "key in" the next page number.

Pages

  • 0:00P100 index. Double-height yellow title on a blue band, a mosaic KINO logo, and a menu whose entries appear one per beat. Fastext keys along the bottom.
  • 0:05P101 signal. The satellite dish as a mosaic picture, panning one block per beat, because teletext can't scroll smoothly.
  • 0:10P102 spectrum. The song's spectrum as 20 block bars with peak hold, coloured like a hi-fi meter.
  • 0:16P888 subtitles. The singer in two-colour mosaic with subtitles in boxed double height: [ MUSIC ], ♪ THE FUTURE ♪, ♪ OF SPEECH ♪, [ VOICES ].
  • 0:21P199 credits, filled in line by line, then held.

Choices

  • Authentic grid, honestly stretched. 40×25 is a 4:3 system; widescreen sets stretched it, so this does too: 64×56 px cells filling the frame.
  • Real mosaic pictures. Photos are converted to 2×3 block cells, ordered-dithered, one foreground colour per cell chosen from the cell's brightness. Whatever detail survives that is what you get.
  • The palette is the palette. Teletext has exactly eight colours, green included. I kept green to the fastext key where it belongs and built pages from blue, cyan, yellow, white and red.
  • On a TV, not a monitor. screenPass in its new colour mode: a shadow-mask grille, curved glass both ways, soft focus at the sides, a little convergence fringe.
  • No flashing text. Teletext had a flash attribute. You'd hate it, so it's not here.

Pieces

  • USEDscreenPass (colour mode, curvature), preparePlate, the spectrum, beatMap.
  • NEWcolorMode on screenPass: keeps the source colours instead of mapping to a ramp, so any colour artwork can go through the CRT.
  • NEWA teletext renderer: page model (cells with character, colours, mosaic bits, double height), a glyph atlas made by rendering a font at 8×14 and thresholding it, and a run-length cell painter.
  • NEWMosaic conversion from any bitmap: photos and type (the KINO logo is Anton rendered tiny and thresholded into blocks).
  • NEWPage loading: rolling header search, then a row-by-row paint over the old page.

What I learned

The first pass garbled every double-height title: a double-height glyph spills into the row below, and drawing row by row meant the next row's background painted over its bottom half. Backgrounds in one pass, glyphs in a second. Classic.

Mosaic pictures live or die by the source. The microphone on a white wall turned into a field of white noise. The satellite dish against a dark sky read instantly as a shape, but its bright face filled in as one solid white blob. Thresholding each block at 50% throws away every mid-tone. An ordered dither (a 4×4 Bayer threshold per block) fixed it: the share of lit blocks now follows brightness, so the dish keeps its shading inside teletext's two-tones-per-cell limit. For this style, pick a bright shape on dark ground, and dither.

Making glyphs by thresholding a real font at teletext resolution works surprisingly well and avoids shipping a pixel font. The result has the chunky verticals and thin horizontals of the real SAA5050 chip without copying it.
